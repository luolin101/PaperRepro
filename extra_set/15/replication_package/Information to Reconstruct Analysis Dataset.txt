This part of the replication file contains five sections. The first is how to merge the dataset that contains the main dependent variable used in the text, as 
well as the dependent variables in the appendix. The second is how to merge the dataset that contains another dependent variable in the text, as well as how
that variable was constructed from several sources. The third is how to merge the dataset that contains the main independent variable and several of the
control variables. The fourth is how to construct the leader year dataset, which implies merging an extant leader year dataset and a country year dataset.
The fifth and final section are instructions on how to construct the Mexico time-series dataset, which includes two variables.

Here is a list of the variable names and their descriptions:

Directed_Credit:
 
Credit by domestic money banks to the government and state-owned enterprises as a percentage of GDP.

Net_Interest_Margin:

The accounting value of banks’ net interest revenue as a share of their interest-bearing assets: ((Interest Returns-Interest Expenses)/Total Earning Assets). 

Return_Bank_Assets:

Commercial banks’ net income to yearly averaged total assets.

Accounting value of banks’ overhead costs as a share of their interest-bearing assets: (Overhead Costs/Total Earning Assets). 

Liquidity: 

Liquid liabilities as a percent of GDP. 

Private_Credit: 

The credit made available by deposit taking banks is operationalized as a percentage of GDP.

Market_Power:

This is the Lerner Index. This is a measure of market power in the banking market that compares output pricing and marginal costs—namely, the markup.

Here is information about the dataset and variables:

The Total_Taxes_GDP variable was made in an Excel spreadsheet using several different sources. Below I explain how I made that variable and the different sources 
used, eventually itemizing each by country.   

I measure total taxation as % GDP on an annual basis for each country in the dataset. Both the numerator and denominator (GDP) are in current local currency units. 
The source for nominal GDP is always the World Bank Development Indicators (WBDI). I downloaded nominal GDP from the WBDI website (GDP (current LCU)) in Excel Form 
and then used an Excel spreadsheet to generate the taxation to GDP ratio by hand. I did it this way because I was using several different sources for the numerator, 
total taxation, and wanted to make sure I did not make a mistake.

The WBDI website is available here, and the data downloads, if selected, into an Excel spreadsheet:
 
http://databank.worldbank.org/data/reports.aspx?source=world-development-indicators

For total taxation, the numerator, the sources vary by country and are discussed and identified below for each country. Total taxation was also compiled in an Excel 
spreadsheet. Because I used several different sources, I copied and pasted the values from each source into the respective cells, one country at a time. This sometimes 
involved keying in values by hand from hardcover books. Other times it meant splicing in values from other Excel spreadsheets that were generated by CD-ROM or were the 
format used in websites where I downloaded the data. I then made the ratios, with nominal GDP as the numerator, by hand, using Excel formulas, in that same spreadsheet. 

The Excel spreadsheet used to generate this variable is also stored here. It contains detailed notes for every single country year observation, including each source 
used and any issues encountered. The Excel spreadsheet is called "TaxationMaster.xls." The Column in which the total taxation variable appears is Column BM of the
first Excel workbook, labeled "Revenues." Note that the ratio is in decimal format; in the Stata file it is in percentage format. This called on replacing the original 
Excel variable using the following command in Stata:

replace Total_Taxes_GDP = Total_Taxes_GDP*100

1. The general sources I used were:

A.	The International Monetary Fund (IMF) Government Finance Statistics Yearbook (GFSY), hardcover edition, various years.
B.	The International Monetary Fund (IMF) Government Finance Statistics Yearbook (GFSY), CD-ROM edition (1972 to 1989).

D.	The Global Development Network Growth Database, Government Finance Series.

F.	The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) Factbook-Statistics, online edition.
G.	The Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) Country Reports, various
H.	The IMF Country Reports, various.

The majority of the data I used for direct taxation was from the first three sources identified above. The reason I pulled data from multiple sources, however, is that
the different IMF datasets was not able to maximize coverage on its own. Nevertheless, I am confident that this variable is reliable and valid across space and time 
because I standardized the tax data from non-IMF sources according to the fiscal categories employed by the IMF’s GFSY as outlined in the GFSY Manual (2001). I 
categorized government revenues using the six major tax groups identified by the IMF and categorized according to their base: 1) income, profits and capital gains; 
2) payroll and workforce; 3) property; 4) goods and services; 5) international trade/transactions; and 6) other taxes. 

Following the IMF, government revenues from income, profits, and capital gains includes taxes on wages, salaries, and other compensation; taxes on interest, dividends,
rent, and royalties; taxes on capital gains and losses on land, securities, and other assets; profits from corporations, partnerships, and sole proprietorships, as well
as from estates and trusts; and taxable income from social security and other insurance schemes (IMF 2001: 50). The data sources I employed are below on a country-by-
country basis.

then copied and pasted this variable from that Excel spreadsheet into the "MexicoCase.dta" file; this meant copying and pasting the data from Excel into the Stata
